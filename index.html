Qué tan abiertas están las personas a hablar de familia y crianza

Le dimos a 3.501 personas uno de dos textos, elegido al azar, y medimos qué cambió en cada una: qué las abre, qué las cierra y a quién le sirve cada manera de contar.

Sección 01 de 08

¿Cómo hablar de familias diversas a quienes no están convencidos?

Esa fue la pregunta. Escribimos dos narrativas que dicen lo mismo de dos maneras distintas, se los dimos a 3.501 personas y medimos qué cambió en cada una. Esto es lo que aprendimos.

Cómo se hizo

Qué es Swayable

Una plataforma para probar mensajes antes de publicarlos. La usan campañas, marcas y organizaciones para saber si un texto, un video o una pieza gráfica de verdad cambia lo que la gente piensa, en vez de adivinarlo.

Por qué esto no es una encuesta

Una encuesta te dice qué piensa la gente. Esto te dice qué la hace cambiar de opinión, y lo mide con el mismo método que se usa para probar un medicamento: se reparte a la gente al azar en grupos, unos reciben el mensaje y otros no, y después se compara.

Qué es el grupo de control

Es el grupo que no recibe nada, y es la pieza que hace que todo funcione. Sin él no sabrías si la gente cambió por tu texto o porque ya venía cambiando. Con él, cualquier diferencia entre los grupos solo puede venir de la narrativa: el azar se encargó de repartir por igual a jóvenes y mayores, creyentes y no creyentes, gente de izquierda y de derecha.

Estas fueron las dos narrativas que testeamos

Son de uso libre: tómalas, córtalas, adáptalas a lo que estés escribiendo.

Narrativa A

Ternura · el mecanismo, sin nombrar

Todos queremos que nuestra familia esté bien.

Por eso, cuando aparece un mensaje que dice que debemos proteger a nuestros hijos o nuestra familia de una amenaza que no conocemos —o no entendemos— es normal sentir miedo, preocupación o incluso ganas de actuar de inmediato.

Y justamente ahí es donde empieza la manipulación.

Algunos mensajes no buscan ayudarnos a entender algo que no conocíamos. Buscan que reaccionemos en lugar de pensar. Porque cuando sentimos que algo cercano está amenazado, es mucho más fácil dejar de hacer preguntas y actuar por impulso.

Hay señales que se repiten en este tipo de mensajes: hablan de una amenaza urgente, usan palabras grandes como "verdad" o "extremismo" para que dudar parezca peligroso, y dan a entender que solo hay dos bandos posibles.

Proteger también significa preguntarnos: ¿Este mensaje me está ayudando a entender algo, o me está empujando a sentir miedo de algo que no entiendo?

Las mejores decisiones no nacen del susto. Nacen cuando buscamos contexto, escuchamos distintas voces y decidimos con calma.

Porque nadie debería decidir por usted cuál es la mejor forma de cuidar a los suyos — ni siquiera quien le está mandando este mensaje.

Lo leyeron 1.005 personas · 212 palabras

Narrativa B

Específica · nombra las técnicas

Todos queremos que nuestra familia esté bien y que nuestros hijos crezcan seguros.

Por eso, cuando alguien dice que hablar de identidad de género es una amenaza a la verdad, o que reconocer a una persona trans es una forma de extremismo, muchos sentimos que debemos tomar partido de inmediato — sin tiempo para entender de qué se está hablando realmente.

Vale la pena mirar de cerca cómo está construido ese mensaje, no solo lo que dice.

Fíjese si usa la palabra "familia" no para describir su propia familia, sino como argumento para decidir qué derechos deberían tener otras familias que no conoce.

Fíjese si le dicen que reconocer que existen personas trans es "ideología" — como si conocer la existencia de alguien fuera lo mismo que imponerle algo a usted.

Y fíjese si el mensaje conecta a personas trans con peligro para niños o mujeres, sin mostrarle un caso concreto, solo la sensación de amenaza.

Esa combinación — urgencia, una palabra sagrada como "familia" o "verdad", y un enemigo señalado sin pruebas — es la misma fórmula que se ha usado en otros temas para que la gente deje de preguntar y empiece a temer.

Usted puede estar en desacuerdo con muchas cosas y aun así negarse a que el miedo decida por usted.

Proteger a su familia y respetar la existencia de otras personas no son cosas opuestas. Nadie debería decidir por usted qué debe sentir sobre alguien que ni siquiera conoce.

Lo leyeron 999 personas · 246 palabras

Hay un acuerdo enorme que no nos esperábamos

Tres de cada cuatro personas creen que cada familia decide lo suyo. Esto no varía por sus ingresos, ni por su edad, ni por su nivel de estudio, ni por la ciudad donde vive o su ideología política.

de las personas dice que las decisiones importantes sobre una familia las debe tomar cada familia según su realidad.

Solo 19,4% quiere que la sociedad ponga normas claras y 5,7% que las instituciones orienten. Pregunta previa a la narrativa · 3.501 personas.

Lo llamativo no es el número: es que no se mueve. Cortamos la muestra de seis maneras distintas y en todas la respuesta se mantuvo entre 68% y 82%.

Entre los diez grupos que salen de cruzar política con género, lo más bajo son los hombres de derecha, con 67,8%, y lo más alto las mujeres de izquierda, con 82,3%. Las mujeres de derecha (79,9%) defienden la autonomía familiar más que los hombres de izquierda (73,8%).

Eso significa

Casi todo el mundo en Colombia está de acuerdo en una cosa: que nadie de afuera le diga a una familia cómo tiene que ser. Ni el gobierno, ni la iglesia, ni el vecino.

Eso vale para la señora católica de Barranquilla y para el muchacho de izquierda en Bogotá casi por igual. Es raro encontrar algo en lo que la gente coincida tanto.

Y es una puerta abierta: si empiezas una conversación por ahí, no estás peleando con nadie todavía. Puedes hablar de familias diversas desde el derecho de cada quien a vivir la suya como quiera, que es una idea que la gente ya tiene.

Sección 04 de 08

Aceptar la diversidad y detectar la manipulación son cosas distintas

Uno esperaría que la gente más abierta sea también la más difícil de engañar. No es así. Hay personas abiertas que se creen cualquier cosa, y personas cerradas con muy buen olfato.

Medimos dos cosas por separado

Qué tan abierta es una persona a las familias diversas. Lo sacamos de tres preguntas: qué define a una familia, cómo reacciona ante argumentos sobre familias distintas, y qué formas de familia reconoce como tales.

Cuánto criterio tiene frente a mensajes que buscan asustarla. Lo sacamos de dos preguntas: qué hace cuando ve en redes un mensaje sobre familia o población LGBTIQ+, y qué considera más importante cuando recibe información que alerta sobre niñez o diversidad.

Después miramos qué características de las personas predicen cada una de las dos. Lo hicimos con un modelo que mete todas las variables al tiempo, para saber cuál pesa de verdad y cuál solo parecía pesar.

Eso significa

Si alguien te dice "los jóvenes son más abiertos", tiene razón. Si te dice "y por eso se dejan engañar menos", está inventando.

Son dos músculos distintos. Uno no viene con el otro.

Comparamos las dos narrativas en nueve resultados distintos, y cada uno dentro de catorce grupos de personas. En total, más de cien comparaciones. Contamos solo las que pasan la prueba estadística, o sea donde la diferencia es lo bastante grande como para descartar el azar.

Dónde gana la A en toda la muestra: en hacer reflexionar (61,6% contra 56,0%) y en enseñar que el miedo se usa para manipular (47,8% contra 40,5%).

Dónde gana con más fuerza: entre quienes defienden la familia tradicional, entre personas evangélicas, entre padres y madres, y en el grupo de 36 a 45 años. Justo los públicos que hay que mover.

Y en un punto la B no solo pierde: en varios de esos grupos quedó por debajo del control. Leerla les dejó menos criterio que no leer nada.

Eso significa

Las dos narrativas cambian opiniones por igual. La diferencia no está en cuánto mueven, sino en a qué costo.

La A llega al mismo lugar sin activar defensas. La B llega igual de lejos, pero por el camino irrita, y en los públicos resistentes hasta bloquea el aprendizaje.

Por eso la respuesta a "¿cuál usamos?" es la A. No porque convenza más, sino porque no cobra peaje.

How do you talk about diverse families to people who aren't convinced?

That was the question. We wrote two narratives that say the same thing in two different ways, gave them to 3,501 people, and measured what changed in each of them. Here is what we learned.

How we did it

What Swayable is

A platform for testing messages before you publish them. Campaigns, brands and organisations use it to find out whether a text, a video or a graphic actually changes what people think, instead of guessing.

Why this isn't a survey

A survey tells you what people think. This tells you what makes them change their mind, and it measures that using the same method used to test a drug: people are split into groups at random, some get the message and some don't, and then you compare.

What the control group is

It's the group that gets nothing, and it's the piece that makes everything else work. Without it you wouldn't know whether people changed because of your text or because they were already shifting. With it, any difference between the groups can only come from the narrative: randomisation spread everyone evenly across young and old, religious and non-religious, left and right.

How this experiment worked

Each person was assigned to one of three groups, without knowing which one they had landed in or that the others existed. Afterwards, everyone answered exactly the same questions.

Narrative A

Tenderness · the mechanism, without naming

We all want our family to be OK.

So when a message turns up saying we have to protect our children or our family from a threat we don't know — or don't understand — it's normal to feel fear, worry, even the urge to act right away.

And that is exactly where the manipulation begins.

Some messages aren't trying to help us understand something we didn't know. They're trying to make us react instead of think. Because when we feel something close to us is under threat, it's far easier to stop asking questions and act on impulse.

There are signs that repeat in this kind of message: they speak of an urgent threat, they use big words like "truth" or "extremism" so that doubting feels dangerous, and they imply there are only two possible sides.

Protecting also means asking ourselves: is this message helping me understand something, or is it pushing me to fear something I don't understand?

The best decisions don't come from fright. They come when we look for context, listen to different voices and decide calmly.

Because no one should decide for you what the best way to care for your own people is — not even whoever sent you this message.

Narrative B

Specific · names the techniques

We all want our family to be OK and our children to grow up safe.

So when someone says that talking about gender identity is a threat to the truth, or that recognising a trans person is a form of extremism, many of us feel we have to pick a side immediately — with no time to understand what is actually being discussed.

It's worth looking closely at how that message is built, not just at what it says.

Notice whether it uses the word "family" not to describe your own family, but as an argument for deciding what rights other families you've never met should have.

Notice whether you're told that acknowledging trans people exist is "ideology" — as if knowing someone exists were the same as imposing something on you.

And notice whether the message links trans people to danger for children or women without showing you a single concrete case, only the feeling of threat.

That combination — urgency, a sacred word like "family" or "truth", and an enemy named without evidence — is the same formula used on other issues to make people stop asking and start fearing.

You can disagree with plenty of things and still refuse to let fear decide for you.

Protecting your family and respecting other people's existence are not opposites. No one should decide for you how to feel about someone you've never even met.

Accepting diversity and spotting manipulation are different things

You'd expect the most open people to also be the hardest to fool. They aren't. There are open people who'll believe anything, and closed people with an excellent nose for it.

We measured two things separately

How open a person is to diverse families. We drew this from three questions: what defines a family, how they react to arguments about different families, and which forms of family they recognise as such.

How much judgment they have when faced with messages designed to frighten them. We drew this from two questions: what they do when they see a message about family or LGBTIQ+ people on social media, and what they consider most important when they get information warning about children or diversity.

Then we looked at which characteristics predict each of the two. We used a model that includes every variable at once, to find out which ones really weigh and which only seemed to.

What this means

If someone tells you "young people are more open", they're right. If they tell you "and that's why they get fooled less", they're making it up.

They're two different muscles. One doesn't come with the other.

Section 08 of 08

How to approach this subject

Eight ways into the conversation, drawn from what the experiment showed. These aren't writing rules: they're decisions about where to start when the person on the other side isn't convinced yet.

01

What isn't named doesn't exist. But not everything gets named upfront

Naming trans people, diversity, gender ideology is necessary and not up for negotiation. What the study shows is when: with people who already agree, or with under-25s, naming it upfront costs nothing. With parents, people over 36 and evangelicals, opening there triggers a defence that shuts the door before you reach your second sentence.

The data The narrative that names the techniques doubled the feeling of "they're trying to talk me into this" in the 36-to-45 group: 21.9% against 10.1%.

02

The enemy is the manipulation, never the neighbour

When the message points at people, people defend themselves. When it points at a tactic — fear, urgency, the invented enemy — people get interested. And there's more: almost nobody is on the side of hate. Hard rejection is 90 people out of 3,501.

The data One in three Colombians isn't against you: they believe their own family model is the right one, or they simply haven't formed a position.

03

Teach the formula, don't fight the case

Explaining how manipulation works — urgency, a sacred word, an enemy without evidence — taught more than denouncing the specific case. In communications this is called pre-bunking: handing someone the tool before the message that wants to frighten them arrives.

The data The narrative describing the mechanism rose 9 points above the control on recognising that fear is used to manipulate. The one naming the case stopped 1.6 points away.

04

Come in through autonomy, where nobody fights

"Each family decides its own business" is the most widely shared idea in the country and it doesn't move by income, age, education, city or ideology. It's the only ground where you can start a conversation without putting anyone on guard.

The data 74.9% across the whole sample. Not a single group drops below 67%, not even right-wing men.

05

Talk about solutions, not recognition

"Solutions for all families" is the only frame that doesn't divide, and it's the second most valued option on the right. "Recognising different kinds of family exist" loses ten points from left to right. You're saying the same thing; one gets in where the other bounces off.

The data Solutions: 25.7% on the left and 21.1% on the right. Recognition: 25.1% and 15.3%.

06

Parents don't lack information: they have too much fear

The audience these campaigns speak to is the most closed in the sample in terms of attitudes. But the way they evaluate information is identical to everyone else's. It isn't a teaching problem: they have more at stake, and that puts them on the defensive.

The data Verifying before concluding: 45.1% without children and 44.6% with children. Zero difference.

07

With young people, change the format before the subject

They're the most open audience and twice as movable as older people. But they're also the ones who consume social causes least and trust their own circle without verifying most. The problem isn't what you tell them: it's in what format and through which channel it reaches them.

The data Between 18 and 25, 66% changed their mind. And only 20.2% consume social causes, against 70.2% who consume memes.

08

Fewer concepts, more stories

A piece has to be understandable by someone who doesn't know the debate, doesn't know the organisations and has never heard of any of this. You don't need to mention "diverse families" or "gender ideology" to show that families are many and that care takes many forms.

The data In the open-ended answers, those who arrived defending the traditional family and then shifted told it through their own experiences, not through concepts.

What this means

This isn't about lowering the tone or hiding anything. It's about choosing where to come in.

The conversation about diverse families is already polarised, and whoever opens it by naming the conflict is speaking to people who already agreed. Whoever opens it through care, autonomy and concrete stories reaches that third of the country with no position yet.

That third is where this is won or lost.

The small print

How we did it. Online experiment with 3,501 people in Colombia's main cities, August 2026. Random assignment to two narratives and a control group. Percentages are weighted; people counts are unweighted. Quotes are verbatim answers, translated from Spanish, with spelling and punctuation corrected.

What this can't tell you. It isn't a nationally representative sample. It can't predict an individual: the models explain between 2% and 13% of why someone answers one way or another. It measures what people say they do, not what they do. Everything was measured right after reading: we don't know what survives a week later. And it tests two long narratives read attentively, not campaign assets.

Both narratives are free to use. Take them, cut them and adapt them to whatever you need.

Among the 1,940 open-ended answers there are 50 people who reject the texts' ideas as a threat to their values. Some use aggressive language towards LGBT people. We don't reproduce them: the relevant fact is that they are 2.6% of the sample, not their literal content.
